# Notes — MTTR/MTBF corpus for ch. 12 (+ ch. 2, ch. 5)

Gathered 2026-07-07 from user-supplied articles + VOID deep-dive. Thesis position: the trend's *engineering core* is right and was scoped by its own originator; the trend's *metric* is statistically dead, killed by the same originator and buried by VOID/Davidovič; what replaces the metric is per-class priced answers — which is Q3 + the recovery triple + the consistency lens. The book doesn't join the trend or fight it; it dissolves it per failure class.

## The corpus, mapped

1. **Allspaw 2010, the origin post** — [MTTR is more important than MTBF (for most types of F)](https://www.kitchensoap.com/2010/11/07/mttr-mtbf-for-most-types-of-f/). Two gifts:
   - **The scope qualifier is IN THE TITLE and everyone dropped it.** "For most types of F" — named exception: failures that shouldn't ever happen, e.g. "accidental data loss." The boundary we derived independently (MTTR inverts for unrecoverable-state classes) is in the origin post. The slogan erased its own scope; the book restores it.
   - **The 2017 self-retraction:** title should have been "TTR is more important than TBF" — the *mean* makes no sense, incidents aren't comparable, start times are negotiable. "Present Allspaw tells past Allspaw: you were wrong, buddy." The VOID critique's seed, planted by the trend's author.
   - Reusable: Jeep vs Rolls-Royce (Bergman) — Jeep = designed-for-repair (Army rebuilds one in <4 min), Rolls = engineered-against-failure, long expensive repairs. Maps onto the ledger: repairability is a *purchased capability with a mechanism*, per component. Paul Hammond: "If you think you can prevent failure, then you aren't developing your ability to respond." Dark launches/flags/percent rollouts as MTTR-first practices (= Q8 deploy-safety mechanisms).
2. **Robbins et al., GameDay** — [USENIX LISA11](https://www.usenix.org/blog/gameday-creating-resiliency-through-destruction), long form: [ACM Queue roundtable "Resilience Engineering: Learning to Embrace Failure"](https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=2371297) (Robbins "Master of Disaster"/Amazon, Krishnan/Google DiRT, Allspaw). Fault injection as *verification of the recovery axis* → belongs to ch. 5/12 (pre-build + in-life SLO verification), not the derivation itself. Hollnagel's four cornerstones (anticipate/monitor/respond/learn) — cite as the socio-technical side. **Boundary to state:** resilience engineering covers the organization; the book covers the system's structure; the recovery axis is where they meet. Don't overclaim into their territory.
3. **Vogels, CACM Vol 63 No 2** — ["Everything Fails All the Time"](https://cacm.acm.org/opinion/everything-fails-all-the-time/) (interview). The ambient/cultural version of the stance; epigraph-grade, analytically thin. Use as the ch. 12 opening line candidate.
4. **Google CRE 2017 (Ross & Brown)** — [Available... or not?](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/gcp/available-or-not-that-is-the-question-cre-life-lessons) — **the most directly book-shaped artifact of the four; slots into ch. 2 and ch. 5 almost verbatim:**
   - Error budget = the priced availability answer: 99.9% over 30 days = 43.2 min of downtime, a concrete planning number. Their revenue-derived target example = "an SLO you haven't priced is a wish," worked.
   - **"Design a system to be as available as is required, but not much more"** = R2/R3 in Google's voice. Independent convergence for the selection rule.
   - Expected downtime = (Period / MTBF) × MTTR — the two-lever arithmetic for ch. 5 budget math; explicit: "we generally target a given availability figure, rather than particular MTBF or MTTR figures," and once the target is met, optimize MTTR ("fast fix"), accepting failure. MTBF/MTTR as *derived planning quantities subordinate to the priced target* — exactly the book's stance.
   - The Shakespeare parable (99.7% server-side "availability" while users see a broken site; black-box probing reveals 80%): measurement must match user-facing operations = the consistency lens's per-operation discipline applied to availability. "Basing your definition of availability on a measurement that does not match user expectations" is the availability version of a one-bit label.

## VOID deep-dive (the metric's obituary)

Sources: [InfoQ — Moving Past Simple Incident Metrics](https://www.infoq.com/articles/incident-metrics-void/), [InfoQ — Analyzing Incident Data across Organizations](https://www.infoq.com/articles/analyzing-incident-data/), [Verica — MTTR is a Misleading Metric, Now What?](https://www.verica.io/blog/mttr-is-a-misleading-metric-now-what/), [SREcon22 — Tales from the VOID](https://www.usenix.org/conference/srecon22americas/presentation/nash).

- **The statistical kill:** incident durations are positively skewed, not normal → central-tendency measures (mean, and also median/mode) misrepresent the data. Nash: "when you think you're saying something about the reliability of your systems, you are using an unreliable metric." Independent confirmation: **Štěpán Davidovič, "Incident Metrics in SRE"** (O'Reilly) — empirical + Monte Carlo: you cannot reliably demonstrate improvement via changes in mean duration. Two independent sources, same conclusion.
- **Duration ≠ severity:** poor correlation — MTTR can improve while impact worsens. (A per-class lens would have predicted this: durations pool incomparable failure classes into one scalar.)
- **Even the raw data is soft:** start/stop times are negotiable attributions (Allspaw's 2017 point, empirically confirmed).
- **Their "instead":** SLOs, socio-technical incident data, incident reviews; explicit caution against replacing one scalar with another scalar — mindset shift, not metric swap.
- **Book synthesis:** VOID's "use SLOs instead" lands exactly on Q3-priced-per-operation. And the statistical lesson unifies with the latency material: **means lie about tails in incident duration for the same reason they lie in latency distributions — the book bans bare -ilities AND bare means; scalars hide scope and shape.** (Ch. 5 + ch. 12 shared thread.)
