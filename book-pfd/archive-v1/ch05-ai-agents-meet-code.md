*Threads: 4 (AI-era coding), 5 (legibility asymmetry), 8 (observable by construction), 15 (standardization)*

## What AI changed and what it didn't

In the past three years, the question of how software gets written has undergone a real change. Code generation that worked in research papers a decade ago now works in production tooling that working developers use every day. Substantial fractions of new code in many teams pass through an AI assistant before reaching review. The output is good enough often enough that the human role has shifted: less typing, more directing; less syntax recall, more specification; less keyboard ergonomics, more judgment about what to ask the model to do and how to verify what it produced.

This is a real change. It is also a smaller change than the most expansive forecasts suggested. Code generation, in the narrow sense of producing syntactically correct source from specifications, was the part of software development with the highest visible activity and the lowest underlying load. The bottleneck of working software has not been typing speed for at least two decades. The bottleneck has been everything around the typing: understanding what to build, deciding how to build it, integrating new behavior with existing systems, maintaining the result as conditions change, transferring knowledge to colleagues, and verifying that what was shipped actually does what the specification implied. AI assistance has made the cheap part cheaper. The expensive parts remain expensive, and in some teams have become relatively more expensive because the cheap part now consumes a smaller share of the total.

A common framing of this shift is that AI is solving software's productivity problem. The framing is incomplete in a specific way: AI has solved a productivity problem that was not the field's load-bearing one. The load-bearing problems — design, maintenance, coordination, knowledge preservation — are now exposed without the typing-cost cover they previously enjoyed. Teams that had been spending substantial time on generation-equivalent work now spend that time elsewhere, and the elsewhere is the layer this book has been describing.

The shift is therefore an opportunity for methodology rather than a replacement for it. The work that AI accelerated was the work methodology had least to say about. The work that remains is exactly the work methodology is for.

## Where AI works well, where it does not

The geography of AI's effectiveness in software development maps cleanly onto the layers described in earlier chapters.

At the substrate layer — protocols, runtimes, formats, standard library calls — AI assistance is highly effective. The substrate has standardized over decades; the model's training data contains millions of examples of substrate usage with substantial consistency across them; producing more substrate-conformant code is exactly what statistical models trained on consistent corpora do well. A developer asking an AI to "set up an HTTP server with a JSON endpoint that calls a database with a parameterized query" gets working code on the first attempt most of the time. The substrate is uniform enough that one statistical estimate of what the code should look like is correct often enough to be useful.

At the tooling layer — formatters, linters, build configurations, deployment definitions — AI assistance is also highly effective. Tooling has standardized inside language ecosystems; conventions are well-documented; the corpus contains many examples that look alike. Asking an AI to "configure CI to run tests and fail on coverage below threshold" produces something close to working on the first attempt for nearly any language.

At the application-vocabulary layer — the structure of business logic, the boundaries between subsystems, the modeling of domain concepts, the composition of workflows — AI assistance is uneven. The corpus the model trained on contains application-layer code from many different teams, none of which share vocabulary with the team currently being assisted. The model produces something plausible based on the average of what it has seen, and the average reflects the field's lack of convergence at this layer. The output is sometimes excellent, sometimes confidently wrong in subtle ways, and consistently uneven across teams whose codebases drifted from the corpus average in different directions. The failure mode is not the model being bad at code. The failure mode is the model encountering a layer where the corpus itself does not yet have a stable answer.

This is the same friction layer that earlier chapters have identified as un-industrialized. AI surfaces the friction because AI scales the encounter. A human engineer joining a team experiences the application-vocabulary layer's idiosyncrasies once, during onboarding, and gradually adapts. An AI assistant never fully amortizes that cost — as models improve through longer context windows, retrieval augmentation, and fine-tuning, adaptation work does not vanish; it shifts form. Ongoing context maintenance, retraining effort, and prompt engineering replace the one-time onboarding tax. Standardization keeps those ongoing costs low and bounded; non-standard codebases make them open-ended, because each improvement in model capability is another opportunity for idiosyncratic structure to exercise its leverage. The distributed cost is more visible than the concentrated one was, and it compounds on any gap the standardization argument has not yet closed.

The natural conclusion to draw from this geography is that AI's effectiveness will continue to follow the standardization gradient. Substrate and tooling, already standardized, get the immediate benefit. The application-vocabulary layer, still un-industrialized, gets uneven benefit until it standardizes. The industrialization of that layer is what changes the AI-effectiveness picture. PFD is one route to that industrialization. Other routes are likely to emerge; whichever ones gain traction will share roughly the same effect on AI-assisted development.

## The fragmentation objection

The most serious objection to the argument so far is that AI changes the standardization arithmetic in the wrong direction. The objection runs: AI lowers the cost of creating non-standard alternatives. A team can now produce, in a fraction of the time it used to take, their own framework, their own conventions, their own tooling — all customized to their specific situation. The cost of going off in your own direction has collapsed. Therefore, the prediction continues, the future is more fragmentation, not less; the application-vocabulary layer will diverge faster, not converge.

The objection is honestly made and partially correct. AI does lower creation cost. Teams can and do produce more bespoke artifacts more quickly than they could three years ago. The visible activity around AI-assisted bespoke creation is real.

The objection's conclusion is incorrect for reasons that take a few moves to surface.

The first move is to distinguish creation cost from maintenance cost. Software's dominant cost has never been creation. Across the working lifetime of any non-trivial codebase, the cost of maintenance — debugging, modifying, extending, integrating, migrating — exceeds the cost of original creation by an order of magnitude or more. AI lowers the creation cost. AI does not lower the maintenance cost in proportion. A bespoke artifact whose creation was cheap because of AI assistance still incurs the maintenance cost of any non-shared system: training new team members on it, transferring it across personnel changes, integrating it with everything else the team depends on, keeping it current as dependencies change. The maintenance cost is paid in personnel time, not in model inference time, and personnel time has not gotten cheaper.

The second move is that network effects compound regardless of creation cost. Markdown beat every custom markup language despite the trivial cost of inventing alternatives. SQL standardized despite continuous vendor incentive to differentiate through extensions. HTTP and JSON and REST won the substrate argument despite no enforcement mechanism. None of these standardizations required high creation cost on the alternatives to succeed; they required only that the cost of *not* converging — training, tooling, hiring, ecosystem fragmentation — exceeded the cost of accepting someone else's already-good-enough specification. AI does not change those convergence forces. If anything, AI amplifies them, because AI tooling itself benefits from standardized substrates and adds another voice to the chorus pulling toward convergence.

The third move is that proliferation precedes convergence. Every industrial standardization that has run its course had a proliferation phase first. Whitney's era had multiple competing methods of producing interchangeable parts before tolerance-based standardization won. Electronics had dozens of bus standards before USB consolidated them. The proliferation phase is not evidence against eventual standardization; it is how standardization happens. Alternatives compete, the field learns which approaches survive contact with scale, the survivors get adopted broadly, the alternatives that did not survive get retired. AI-era proliferation at the application-vocabulary layer is consistent with — perhaps a normal part of — the industrialization the layer is undergoing. The proliferation phase is what we are watching now. The convergence phase follows.

The fourth move is a category distinction the objection elides. An "alternative to an existing standard" is one kind of artifact; a "first attempt at a cost column that has no standard yet" is a different kind. AI-assisted bespoke work at the application-vocabulary layer is mostly the second kind. There is no existing standard for that layer to defect from. Teams producing their own conventions are not fragmenting away from a settled standard; they are participating in the proliferation that precedes settlement. The accounting of "fragmentation" looks different once that distinction is in view.

The objection points at real activity. The conclusion it draws from that activity does not survive examination. The activity is consistent with a layer in its proliferation phase, with the standardization-favoring forces (maintenance cost, network effects, hiring pressure, tooling alignment) running underneath. None of those forces have weakened in the AI era. Several of them have strengthened.

There is a subtler pressure the objection misses entirely. Current AI inherits the corpus it was trained on, and that corpus is overwhelmingly entity-first. Teams adopting process-first conventions find themselves overriding AI suggestions on the patterns where their vocabulary diverges most from the training-data mean. That override cost is real — it is friction, paid in attention and correction cycles, every session. But it is also a feedback signal. Conventions that teams actually adopt, correct toward, and commit against do eventually appear in the corpus. The feedback loop is slow and noisy; it does not move on any team's timeline. But it is not absent. Every team that consistently pushes against the entity-first default and commits the alternative is contributing, slowly, to shifting what the next generation of models treats as the center of gravity. The standardization argument does not weaken here. It extends.

## What benefits humans benefits AI

The most useful observation about AI's relationship to methodology is also the simplest. The structural properties that make code legible to a human reviewer — clear naming, consistent vocabulary, explicit type structure, predictable composition, well-isolated effects, named patterns — are the same structural properties that make code tractable to an AI assistant. There is no separate set of AI-friendly conventions distinct from human-friendly conventions. Where the conventions diverge in any specific recommendation, the divergence usually reflects a flaw in the recommendation rather than a real difference between the audiences.

This is an observation, not an engineering target. PFD was not designed for AI. It was designed for the human reading the code — the teammate, the auditor, the successor, the engineer reviewing changes during an on-call rotation. The AI compatibility falls out as a consequence: anything legible to that human reader, structured precisely enough that another human can verify it, is also code an AI assistant can navigate, modify, and reason about with relatively few surprises. The methodology happens to satisfy AI's structural requirements because it satisfies the human structural requirements that AI inherits.

The observation matters because it inverts a common framing in the AI-tooling discourse. The common framing treats AI as a new audience with its own requirements that teams need to optimize for. The inversion is that AI inherits the requirements humans already had. Teams optimizing for legibility, predictability, and structural consistency for human reasons end up with codebases AI handles well as a side effect. Teams trying to optimize specifically for AI without those human-facing goals tend to produce code that is neither human-legible nor reliably AI-tractable — because the specific signals AI uses to navigate code are the signals that human-legibility discipline was already producing.

This is a friendlier framing of the AI-methodology relationship than the discourse usually provides. It does not require teams to choose between AI-readiness and human-readiness. The two are the same readiness with different beneficiaries. A methodology that scales for human teams is a methodology that scales for AI-augmented teams, with the same structural commitments serving both. The choice teams have to make is whether to make those structural commitments at all — not which audience to make them for.

## What stays human

The work that AI accelerated was the part of software development that was most mechanical, most subject to learned patterns, most amenable to statistical approximation from large corpora. The work that remains was the work AI had the least leverage on to begin with: deciding what to build, understanding the domain well enough to model it correctly, navigating organizational constraints, communicating with stakeholders who do not read code, making the architectural trade-offs that depend on knowing things about the situation that no corpus contains.

This is the work that has always been the interesting work. It is also the work that most engineers report wanting more of. The honest read of AI's effect on software development, taken at the population level rather than the headline level, is that AI moved the boundary between mechanical work and judgment work in a direction that gives engineers more of what they already wanted and less of what they already wanted less of. The distribution of work has shifted in a direction most working developers, asked carefully and given time to think, would have shifted it themselves if they could.

The threat in the AI discourse is the framing that suggests this shift is at engineers' expense. The framing is incorrect in the direction the data actually supports. Engineers whose work has shifted toward design and judgment generally report higher satisfaction with the work, not lower. The engineers reporting frustration with AI assistance are usually frustrated with specific aspects of the tooling — uneven quality, the cost of verifying generated code, the difficulty of getting the AI to understand idiosyncratic conventions — not with the change in the shape of the work itself.

PFD is in this sense aligned with the direction the work is moving. It treats design and judgment as the activities worth investing in. It treats the mechanical layer as something to be standardized and offloaded — to tooling, to AI assistance, to convention — rather than as the activity that defines the craft. The methodology was not designed to support that direction; it was designed before the direction was as visible as it is now. The alignment is incidental. It is also welcome.

## What the AI era requires

The argument of this chapter is small in claim and load-bearing in implication. The claim is that AI changed the surface of software development without changing its underlying structure, that the application-vocabulary layer where the structure lives is in the proliferation phase of standardization rather than the divergence phase, and that the methodology requirements for working well with AI are the same methodology requirements that working well without AI already had.

What follows from this, for teams choosing how to operate in the current environment, is straightforward. The methodology investment that would have been worthwhile five years ago is more worthwhile now, because AI raises the value of the structural properties methodology produces. The teams whose code is legible, whose vocabulary is consistent, whose patterns are recognizable, get more leverage from AI assistance than the teams whose codebases are idiosyncratic. The investment compounds. The structural commitments pay off twice: once in human productivity, once in AI productivity, with the same commitments serving both.

The remaining chapters of this book describe the specific structural commitments and the methodology that produces them. The book was written for human readers and human teams. That it also describes a methodology AI handles unusually well is not the book's premise. It is a consequence of writing the book for the audience that has always been the right one — the human reader, present and future, who has to make the code work.
